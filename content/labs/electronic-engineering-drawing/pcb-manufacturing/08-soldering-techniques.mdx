# Soldering Techniques for Through-Hole Components

## Overview

Soldering is the fundamental skill for assembling electronic circuits. A proper solder joint provides both electrical connectivity and mechanical strength. This laboratory session covers through-hole soldering techniques, safety procedures, solder joint inspection criteria, and common soldering defects.

## Objective

To understand soldering safety procedures and equipment setup, perform through-hole component soldering on a practice PCB, create reliable solder joints with proper wetting and fillet formation, inspect and evaluate solder joint quality according to IPC-A-610 standards, and identify and correct common soldering defects.

## Theory and Engineering Concepts

Soldering is the process of joining two metal surfaces using a filler metal (solder) with a melting point below 450°C. The key concepts include:

- **Wetting**: The ability of molten solder to spread evenly over a metal surface, forming a intermetallic bond. Good wetting requires clean surfaces and proper flux application.
- **Flux**: A chemical cleaning agent that removes oxidation from metal surfaces, promoting solder wetting. Flux can be rosin-based (RMA), water-soluble, or no-clean.
- **Intermetallic Compound (IMC)**: A thin layer of Cu₆Sn₅ that forms between the copper pad and the solder, creating the electrical and mechanical bond.
- **Solder Joint Geometry**: A proper through-hole solder joint should have:
  - A concave fillet on both the top and bottom of the PCB
  - Solder that penetrates completely through the hole
  - A smooth, shiny surface finish
  - Contact angle less than 90 degrees between solder and pad

## Project / Task

Solder 10 through-hole resistors and 5 IC sockets onto a practice PCB. Each joint must meet IPC-A-610 Class 2 acceptance criteria.

## Procedure / Implementation

### Step 1: Safety and Setup

1. Set up the soldering station:
   - Place soldering iron in the stand
   - Ensure fume extractor is positioned near the work area
   - Have wet sponge or brass wool ready for tip cleaning
2. Set iron temperature:
   - For leaded solder (63/37): **315-350°C** (600-660°F)
   - For lead-free solder: **350-370°C** (660-700°F)
3. Safety precautions:
   - Work in a well-ventilated area
   - Wear safety glasses
   - Do not touch the iron tip (temperatures exceed 300°C)
   - Wash hands after handling solder (lead content)

### Step 2: Tip Preparation

1. Clean the soldering iron tip on wet sponge or brass wool
2. Apply a small amount of solder to the tip (tinning):
   - This improves heat transfer
   - Protects the tip from oxidation
3. Inspect the tip for damage or oxidation
4. If tip is dark or pitted, use tip tinner to restore

### Step 3: Insert Component

1. Bend resistor leads at 90 degrees to match PCB hole spacing
2. Insert component leads through the PCB holes from the top
3. Component body should sit flush against the PCB surface
4. For IC sockets, ensure correct orientation (notch alignment)
5. Bend the leads outward slightly on the bottom to hold the component in place

### Step 4: Soldering Technique — The Five-Step Method

1. **Clean**: Clean the iron tip on the sponge
2. **Heat**: Touch the iron tip to both the pad and component lead simultaneously
3. **Feed**: Feed solder wire into the joint opposite the iron tip
4. **Flow**: Solder should melt and flow into the joint within 2-3 seconds
5. **Remove**: Remove solder wire first, then remove the iron

### Step 5: Inspect Solder Joint

1. Visual inspection criteria (IPC-A-610 Class 2):
   - **Fillet**: Concave shape, completely wetting the pad and lead
   - **Coverage**: 360-degree wetting around the lead
   - **Fill**: Solder visible on both top and bottom of the board
   - **Surface**: Smooth and shiny
   - **Contact Angle**: Less than 90 degrees
2. Acceptable defects (minor):
   - Slight dewetting (less than 10% of pad area)
   - Minor solder balls (less than 0.5mm, non-adjacent to conductors)
3. Unacceptable defects (must rework):
   - Cold joint (dull, grainy appearance)
   - Insufficient wetting
   - Solder bridge between adjacent pads
   - Disturbed joint (solidified but moved)
   - Excess solder (bulbous joint)

### Step 6: Component Trimming

1. After soldering, trim excess component leads:
   - Use flush cutters
   - Cut as close to the solder joint as possible
   - Ensure no sharp protrusions remain
2. Dispose of trimmed leads safely

### Step 7: Cleaning

1. Clean flux residue from the PCB:
   - Use isopropyl alcohol and a lint-free brush
   - Gently scrub the soldered areas
   - Allow to dry completely
2. For water-soluble flux: Rinse with deionized water

## What Was Done

Successfully soldered 10 resistors and 5 IC sockets onto a practice PCB using the five-step soldering method. All 20 solder joints passed IPC-A-610 Class 2 visual inspection criteria with proper fillet formation, complete pad wetting, and shiny surface finish. Two joints required rework due to insufficient solder — these were corrected by adding flux and reflowing. Final flux residue was cleaned using isopropyl alcohol.
